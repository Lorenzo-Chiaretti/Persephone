# POI 03 — Santo Stefano Basin: Transporting the Duomo Marble

**Type:** Inner harbor basin (minor dock)
**Coordinates:** 45.4608° N, 9.1967° E
**Main historical period:** 1386–ca. 1500
**AR Tags:** `#duomo` `#marble` `#candoglia` `#basin` `#construction`

---

## What the Santo Stefano Basin Was

The **Santo Stefano Basin** was not a natural lake. It was an artificial basin — a small urban dock — excavated in the 14th century as an arrival and distribution point for goods transported by water into the heart of Milan.

It stood in the area corresponding today to **Largo Augusto** and **Via Verziere**, only a few hundred meters from the Duomo construction site. It took its name from the nearby **Basilica of Santo Stefano Maggiore**, one of the oldest churches in the city (its origins date back to the 5th century).

The basin was directly connected to the **Navigli inner canal ring** through a canal known as the **Naviglio di Santo Stefano** (or *Navigliaccio*), which entered the city from the east through Porta Orientale, today’s Porta Venezia.

---

## The Duomo and the Marble Problem

On **May 30, 1386**, Duke **Gian Galeazzo Visconti** laid the foundation stone of the Milan Cathedral. Or rather, the foundation stone was placed on a construction site that already existed. But the true challenge was not architectural: it was logistical.

The Milan Cathedral is built almost entirely from **Candoglia marble**, a pink-white marble quarried in **Candoglia**, in the Verbano area (today part of the Province of Verbano-Cusio-Ossola), more than 100 kilometers from Milan.

Transporting these enormous masses of stone — each block weighing as much as **5 to 8 tons** — across the Po Valley was a colossal undertaking. The solution was the Navigli canal system.

---

## The Marble Route: From Candoglia to the Duomo

The journey of each marble block lasted on average **2 to 4 weeks**, depending on weather conditions and canal traffic.

### Phase 1 — The Quarries (Candoglia, Ossola Valley)

The blocks were extracted by hand using wedges and mallets. Stonecutters marked each block with the insignia of the **Veneranda Fabbrica del Duomo di Milano**: the initials **AUF** (*Ad Usum Fabricae*), which exempted the cargo from all tolls and customs duties throughout the duchy, an extraordinary privilege granted by Gian Galeazzo in 1387.

### Phase 2 — Lake Maggiore

The blocks were loaded onto **rafts** (large platforms made of bound logs) and dragged toward the lake. They were then transferred onto flat-bottomed cargo barges (*barche luganesi* or *luganèe*) capable of carrying up to 30–40 tons of freight.

### Phase 3 — The Ticino River

From Lake Maggiore, the vessels traveled downstream along the **Ticino River** to **Tornavento** (near Turbigo), where the artificial **Naviglio Grande** began.

### Phase 4 — The Naviglio Grande

The artificial canal, excavated between 1177 and 1272, carried water — and with it the boats — from Tornavento to the **Darsena of Porta Ticinese**. The route covered approximately 50 kilometers. The current favored eastbound transport, but marble barges were often hauled from the riverbank by men or oxen using ropes, a practice known as **tow-hauling**.

### Phase 5 — The Inner Canal Ring and the Basin

From the Darsena, the boats entered the inner canal ring, followed the canal eastward, and ascended through the locks to the Santo Stefano Basin. Here the marble blocks were unloaded and transported either manually or on ox carts to the Duomo construction site, less than 500 meters away.

---

## The Basin as the Duomo’s Harbor

Between **1386 and roughly 1500**, the Santo Stefano Basin was Milan’s most important harbor for construction materials. The archives of the **Veneranda Fabbrica del Duomo di Milano** — one of the most complete documentary collections in Europe for this period — record incoming cargoes in extraordinary detail:

* Type and weight of the marble blocks
* Name of the boatman and vessel
* Date of arrival and delivery to the construction site
* Payment made to the transporter

The records show that during peak periods (1390–1410), as many as **15 to 20 marble barges per month** arrived at the basin.

---

## Technical Specifications of the Basin

* **Estimated surface area:** approximately 4,000–5,000 m²
* **Depth:** 1.8–2.2 meters (sufficient for marble-laden barges)
* **Quays:** brick and stone embankments with **iron mooring rings** embedded into the walls
* **Construction cranes:** visual evidence from 15th-century illuminated manuscripts depicts **manual jib cranes** (rotating beam cranes operated by hand winches) used to unload the heaviest blocks
* **Cart access:** the southern bank connected to a paved inclined ramp facilitating the movement of stone blocks onto wagons

---

## Boats and Boatmen

The vessels used to transport the Duomo marble were highly specialized:

* **Luganese barges** (*navili*): 20–25 meters long, 4–5 meters wide, with completely flat bottoms designed for shallow waterways. Built from larch or chestnut wood, with reinforced hulls to support the weight of the marble.
* **Pole-propelled navigation:** in the shallow waters of the inner canals, boatmen used long wooden poles to push against the canal bed. Oars were unusable in many narrow sections.
* **Crew:** 3–6 men per vessel. The captain (*el patron*) was responsible for the cargo and negotiations with Duomo officials.

Most of the Duomo marble boatmen originated from the **lake districts** (Lake Maggiore and Lake Como): communities of inland navigators whose expertise was passed down from father to son. Many settled permanently in villages along the Naviglio Grande.

---

## The Atmosphere Around the Basin

The basin district was among the liveliest — and most chaotic — areas of medieval Milan.

At dawn, when the barges arrived, the neighborhood suddenly came alive: **marble blocks were unloaded using chains and winches**, amid the deafening clatter of iron and stone. Supervisors from the Fabbrica inspected each block with hammer and chisel, rejecting those with flawed veins or frost cracks.

Street vendors on the quay sold bread, cheese, cured meats, and wine to the boatmen. A small tavern overlooked the water directly — municipal records already mention it in 1402 as *“the tavern of the Navigliaccio.”*

The white and pink marble, still wet from the journey, gleamed in the morning sunlight. Residents of the district became so accustomed to the sight that they informally referred to the area simply as ***“the quarry,”*** as though the Candoglia quarry itself had moved into the city.

---

## The End of the Basin

As construction on the Duomo slowed toward the end of the 15th century and canal traffic gradually declined, the basin lost its primary function. During the **16th and 17th centuries**, it was progressively filled with debris, waste, and landfill soil.

By the late 17th century it had already become largely silted up. Its edges were subdivided and built over. In **1818**, the area was definitively urbanized with the redesign of Largo Augusto.

A final fragment of the connecting canal (*Navigliaccio*) remained open until the general covering of Milan’s canals in **1929–1930**.

---

## Quick Fact

> **Did you know?** The **AUF** mark — *Ad Usum Fabricae* — carved into every block of Candoglia marble guaranteed exemption from all customs duties throughout the Duchy of Milan. This privilege was granted by Gian Galeazzo Visconti in 1387 and formally remained in effect for centuries. The **Veneranda Fabbrica del Duomo di Milano** still exists today and is one of the oldest continuously operating private institutions in Europe.
