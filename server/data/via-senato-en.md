# POI 01 — Via Senato: The Navigli Ring Canal and Its Burial

## General Historical Context

Today, Via Senato runs above what was once the eastern section of the **Navigli Ring Canal** (*Cerchia dei Navigli*), the system of canals that encircled medieval Milan like a broad navigable ring of water.

This canal was not built for decoration. It was the logistical backbone of a city that, during the Middle Ages and the Renaissance, depended on water for survival.

The Navigli Ring followed the route of the ancient Roman walls and later the medieval fortifications. Excavation of the canal began in **1157**, under the government of the Commune of Milan, in order to feed the defensive moats and connect the river systems of the Seveso and Olona rivers.

---

## Construction and Expansion

The section corresponding to modern Via Senato was part of the **Inner Moat** (*Fossa interna*), completed in its definitive form by the middle of the 13th century.

In **1438**, the canals of the Ring were integrated with the **Naviglio della Martesana**, which carried waters from the Adda River into the heart of the city. This connection transformed Milan into one of the most sophisticated hydraulic hubs in Europe.

During the 15th century, **Filippo Maria Visconti** and later the **Sforza dynasty** invested heavily in maintaining and improving the system. Under **Ludovico Sforza**, **Leonardo da Vinci** actively worked on the engineering of Milan’s canals, introducing the system of mitre-gate locks (see POI 02).

---

## Technical Specifications of the Canal

* **Average width of the canal along Via Senato:** approximately 15–18 meters
* **Water depth:** 1.5–2.5 meters depending on season and maintenance
* **Total length of the complete Ring Canal:** approximately 6.3 km
* **Managed elevation gradient:** the Ring Canal descended slightly from northwest to southeast, exploiting the natural slope of the glacial soil

The embankments were reinforced with **exposed brickwork**, still visible today in surviving sections of the Ring Canal (for example around Via Carrobbio). The bridges crossing the canal were made of stone or timber, with spans wide enough for the passage of **flat-bottomed barges** known as *barconi* or *navili*.

---

## What the Boats Transported

The vessels traveling through the Via Senato section primarily carried:

* **Candoglia marble** destined for the construction of the Milan Cathedral (see POI 03 for details)
* **Grain and flour** from the mills of the Lombard countryside
* **Timber** from the Lecco area, floated downstream along the Adda River
* **Coal** and construction materials
* **Luxury goods** (silk, spices, Venetian glass) during periods of peak commercial prosperity

Traffic was particularly intense at dawn: boatmen departed from the Santo Stefano Basin or from the Darsena with the aid of the gentle current.

---

## Life Along the Canal Banks

The canal banks in this section were animated by washerwomen, craftsmen, and merchants. The **washerwomen** — known in the Milanese dialect as *lavandere* — occupied the shores from early morning, beating clothes against flat stones placed along the water’s edge.

The characteristic smell of the district was that of **wet wool** and **canal mud**, mixed with smoke from the chimneys of the noble palaces overlooking the water. During summer, the canal produced even stronger odors: the waters collected waste from the surrounding alleys.

Milanese residents also used the canal banks for dumping household refuse, leading to periodic municipal decrees. In **1498**, the city government issued an ordinance prohibiting the throwing of “garbage and carcasses” into the waters of the Ring Canal, under penalty of a fine of 25 soldi.

---

## The Burial of the Canal: 1929–1930

The decision to cover the Navigli Ring Canal was taken during the Fascist period, as part of the **1926 Master Plan for Milan**. Mayor **Luigi Mangiagalli** and later civic administrator **Giuseppe Marcora** oversaw the operation.

The burial works affecting **Via Senato** and neighboring streets (Via Francesco Sforza, Corso di Porta Vittoria, Via Molino delle Armi) were carried out between **1929 and 1930**.

The official reasons were hygienic — the canal had become an open-air sewer — and infrastructural, since growing automobile traffic required wider roads. However, the decision was already controversial at the time: urban planners and intellectuals such as **Luca Beltrami** had protested against the first canal burials in 1919–1920.

Beneath the asphalt of Via Senato, the bed of the ancient canal still physically exists. The area was never entirely reclaimed: the foundations of several buildings still rest upon the original brick embankments.

---

## Quick Fact

> **Did you know?** Beneath Via Senato there is still, buried under the asphalt, the **brick vault** constructed to cover the canal during the works of 1929. In several locations, road excavations have uncovered fragments of wooden boats and medieval amphorae. The Ring Canal also had another popular name: Milanese citizens simply called it *“el fosso”* — “the ditch.”
