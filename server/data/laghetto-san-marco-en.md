## Historical Context: The Problem of Water Level Differences

Navigating through Milan in the 15th century meant facing a significant engineering challenge: the city’s canals were not all at the same elevation.

The **Naviglio Grande** (coming from Abbiategrasso and the Ticino River) and the **Naviglio della Martesana** (descending from the Adda River) stood at different levels compared to the inner canal ring. To move a boat from one canal to another, it had to be literally raised or lowered.

The solution was the **navigation lock**: water chambers sealed by movable gates that functioned as hydraulic elevators for boats.

---

## Leonardo da Vinci and the Mitre Gates

The **Conca dell'Incoronata**, located along the Naviglio della Martesana near present-day Via San Marco, is directly associated with the engineering work of **Leonardo da Vinci** (1452–1519).

Leonardo arrived in Milan in **1482**, serving **Ludovico Sforza**. Beyond paintings and war machines, he devoted extensive study to hydraulics. His notes on Milan’s canals fill dozens of pages of the *Codex Atlanticus* (preserved at the Ambrosian Library).

Between **1496 and 1497**, Leonardo designed and likely supervised the construction of locks with **double-leaf gates** (known as *mitre gates* or *Leonardesque gates*) along the Naviglio della Martesana. The principle was simple yet revolutionary for its time:

1. The boat entered the **lock chamber** (a rectangular basin sealed by two pairs of gates).
2. The upstream gates were closed.
3. Through **small sluice openings** (*wickets* or *sluice ports*) built into the gates themselves, water slowly flowed out or in, equalizing the level inside the chamber with that of the destination canal.
4. The downstream gates opened, and the boat exited.

---

## Technical Specifications of the Lock

* **Length of the inner chamber:** approximately 35–40 meters (sufficient for the largest boats in use at the time)
* **Width:** approximately 8–9 meters
* **Managed elevation difference:** variable, but estimated around **1.5–2.5 meters** in the San Marco area
* **Operating time:** approximately 20–30 minutes for complete filling or emptying of the chamber
* **Gate materials:** solid **oak beams** reinforced with wrought-iron bands; the hinges were forged in iron and anchored to pillars made of Viggiù stone

The gates were not vertical like modern ones, but angled to form an **open V-shape** facing the current (a system known as *mitre gate* design). This geometry used the pressure of the water itself to keep the gates closed: the greater the difference in water level, the more tightly the gates pressed against one another.

**Leonardo described this principle in the Codex Atlanticus (folio 656r):** the gates resist hydrostatic pressure not because of brute strength, but because of their shape.

## Why It Was Called “dell’Incoronata”

The name derives from the **Oratory of the Incoronata**, a small religious building that once stood near the lock. During the Middle Ages and Renaissance, sacred buildings were often placed near critical waterways: boatmen would stop to pray before attempting the ascent maneuver, which carried real risks such as gate failure or sudden flooding of the chamber.

The **Church of Santa Maria Incoronata**, still standing today at Corso Garibaldi 116, was founded in 1451 by **Francesco Sforza** for his wife **Bianca Maria Visconti**. Its double façade, unique in Milan, incorporates two distinct buildings constructed in sequence. Its proximity to the lock was no coincidence.

## The Sound and Atmosphere of the Lock

Contemporary witnesses described the lock area as one of the loudest and liveliest places in Milan.

The **creaking of the oak gates** could be heard from afar: poorly lubricated iron hinges shrieked whenever the gates opened. Boatmen shouted orders to one another across the chamber. The **lock keepers** — municipal employees responsible for operating the system — collected a **transit toll** directly on the canal bank.

Waiting for the lock to open inevitably created queues: boats remained tied to the **iron mooring rings** embedded in the canal walls, while sailors went ashore, lit fires, and cooked polenta in copper cauldrons. The smell of soaked timber, stagnant water, and wood smoke defined every lock in the city.

In winter, the main problem was **ice**: gates became jammed, and sluice mechanisms froze solid. Municipal archives record periods when navigation was completely halted for weeks during the harsh winters of the 16th and 17th centuries in the Po Valley.

---

## The Lock After Leonardo

The lock remained operational for more than four centuries and underwent several renovations. In **1819**, during Austrian rule, the wooden gates were rebuilt using a more advanced hydraulic distribution system.

The section of the Naviglio della Martesana that crossed the city was progressively covered between **1929 and 1930**, together with the inner canal ring. The lock ceased operations and was demolished. Today, the area is occupied by Via San Marco and the surrounding buildings.

A **surviving lock** of similar design can still be seen in Milan: the **Conca di Viarenna**, located on Via Conca del Naviglio, survived the canal covering because it had been partially buried and was rediscovered during restoration works in the 1990s.

## Quick Fact

> **Did you know?** Leonardo da Vinci did not invent the navigation lock — it already existed in the Netherlands in the 14th century — but he perfected the V-shaped gate system that remains the standard design for locks worldwide today, from the Panama Canal to Venice’s waterways. When you visit a modern lock, you are looking at a solution refined in Milan in 1496.

## The Complete System of Milanese Locks

Leonardo and his successors built or improved at least **six locks** along Milan’s canal network:

* Conca dell'Incoronata (Naviglio della Martesana, San Marco district)
* Conca di Viarenna (Naviglio Grande / inner canal ring, Carrobbio district) — **still existing**
* Conca di Porta Ticinese (exit toward the Naviglio Grande)
* Conca di Cassina de' Pomm (eastern entrance to the Martesana canal)
* Conca Fallata (along the Naviglio Pavese, outside the city)
* Conca di San Marco (a second, smaller lock in the same area)

Each lock had a resident **lock keeper**, a small maintenance house, and a transit register. These records, now preserved at the State Archives of Milan, are a valuable source for the history of Lombard trade.

In the AR layer positioned around Via San Marco / Corso Garibaldi, you can see:

* **The 3D reconstruction of the lock chamber:** a 38-meter-long rectangular basin with red Lombard brick walls and a river-stone floor. The water inside is still and opaque, slightly green from algae.
* **Animated Leonardesque gates:** the two pairs of gates slowly open and close. Notice the V-shape and the small rectangular sluice openings that open before the main gates to equalize the water level. The animation reproduces a full 25-minute cycle accelerated into 30 seconds.
* **The ascending barge:** a cargo barge loaded with white marble (see POI 03) enters through the lower gate. The water level slowly rises around the hull. When the upper gate opens, the vessel continues toward the Laghetto.
* **The lock keeper:** a 3D Renaissance character (dark cloak, wide-brimmed hat) records the transit in a ledger book. If you interact with him, he reads aloud an archival entry from 1503: *“On the 4th day of March, one marble boat for the Duomo, toll paid, 8 soldi.”*
